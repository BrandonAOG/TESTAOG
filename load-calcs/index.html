📖 How to Use This Calculator

Overview

Performs a Residential Optional Load Calculation per NEC 2023, Art. 220.82 for single-family dwellings. Complete Steps 1–5 in order, then view results on the Summary tab.

The Optional Method sums all loads, then applies a demand factor: first 10,000 VA @ 100%, remainder @ 40%.

Step 1 General Lighting, Small Appliance & Laundry

Enter the finished floor area (sq ft). NEC requires 3 VA/sq ft, minimum 2 small appliance circuits (1,500 VA ea.) and 1 laundry circuit (1,500 VA). These floors cannot be reduced.

Step 2 HVAC

Select CU/HP tonnage and strip heat for each AHU. The NEC uses the larger of: (a) heat element × its demand factor + blower @ 100%, vs (b) CU/HP load + blower @ 100%. The blower is never derated — always counts at 100%.

The blower is derived from the tonnage, not chosen separately: it belongs to the air handler, and a 5-ton unit moves more air than a 2-ton one no matter what heater kit is in it. PSC figures run 450 VA at 1 ton to 1,050 VA at 5 ton, and the row shows the value it used. Mini splits contribute none — the indoor head's fan is already inside that unit's nameplate. Gas furnace, hydronic coil, or an air handler with no strips = leave heat on "None / gas heat"; the blower still counts, and the unit is not counted as electric space heating.

The heat demand factor follows NEC 220.82(C)(4) and (C)(5): 65% for fewer than four separately controlled electric space heating units, 40% for four or more. At exactly four units the 40% factor applies. Only AHUs with an actual electric heat element count toward that total — an AHU set to "GAS + blower," or one with a condenser and no heat strips, is not a heating unit. The Step 2 "Demand Factor" chip shows the live unit count and which subsection is being applied.

Step 3 Fixed Appliances

EV chargers: counted at nameplate, per 220.82(B)(3). The 7,200 VA minimum in 220.57 is part of the standard method (Art. 220 Part III) — this calculator runs the optional method (Part IV), which is an alternative to Part III, not an addition to it. 220.82 imports only 220.61, by name, for the neutral. The 125% continuous-load factor in 625.41 sizes the branch circuit and does not belong in this calculation either.

All permanently installed appliances. Rename, set qty, enter VA by selecting from the list or typing a custom value. Set voltage (120V or 240V) — this affects the neutral calculation. Gas checkbox removes the load. No separate demand factor (Optional Method). The Pool Equipment Panel row here represents the feeder VA going to the pool sub-panel.

Step 4 Dryers

Add as many dryers as needed. Check Gas to exclude any (250 VA of controls counts). Enter the nameplate rating — that is what 220.82(B)(3)c asks for. 5,000 VA is the common size, not a floor: the 5,000 VA minimum in 220.54 belongs to the standard method (Art. 220 Part III), and the optional method in Part IV is an alternative to Part III, not an addition to it. A compact dryer nameplated below 5,000 VA counts at its nameplate.

Step 5 Cooking

Add cooktops and ovens/ranges individually. Each can be marked gas (excluded). Wattage selectable from 5,000–12,500 W in 500 W steps, or type a custom value. The "All Gas Kitchen" checkbox zeros all cooking at once. The raw VA is added directly to the demand pool — no Col C demand factor applies under the Optional Method (NEC 220.82).

Pool Panel Tab

Sizes the feeder from the main panel to the pool sub-panel. The whole panel is taken at 125%. 430.24 would allow 125% on the largest motor plus 100% of the rest, but pool equipment runs for hours at a time, so every leg is treated as a continuous load under 215.2(A)(1). That is always at or above the 430.24 minimum, and it is a rule a tech can repeat without arithmetic. The Phase A / Phase B amps shown are raw — what the panel actually sees — and the feeder wire and panel rating carry the 125%. 120V loads (lights) appear on Phase A only; all 240V loads split evenly.

Step 3 takes the pool panel at nameplate, 100%. The 125% is a conductor-sizing rule and does not belong in a 220.82 load calculation — the same reason the EV charger is counted at nameplate here rather than at its 625.41 branch-circuit figure. Sizing the wire and stating the load are two different questions, and only the wire gets the multiplier.

Conductor Sizing

Choose the feeder sizing table in Configuration. NEC 310.12 applies to 1&2 family dwelling services rated 100A–400A (uses the 83% loading column). NEC 310.16 (75°C) is the standard ampacity table used for all other feeders and branch circuits. The neutral is always sized from 310.16. Aluminum is code-compliant and common for service entrance conductors. The EGC is sized per NEC Table 250.122 based on the OCPD rating of the circuit it protects.

The grounding electrode conductor is a different conductor and is sized differently — Table 250.66, from the largest ungrounded service conductor, not the OCPD. Where the service is run in parallel, Note 1 to the table applies: the input is the summed area of the corresponding conductors, so two sets of 1/0 (211,200 cmil) lands on the 4/0 row, not the 1/0 row.

Tick the electrodes actually present. Three of them permit a smaller conductor on their own run: 250.66(A) caps a rod, pipe or plate run at 6 AWG copper (4 AWG aluminum), 250.66(B) caps a concrete-encased electrode at 4 AWG copper, and 250.66(C) caps a ground ring at the ring conductor itself. A metal water pipe or building steel gets no reduction — those runs take the full table size.

All three caps are written to cover the grounding electrode conductor or bonding jumper, and 250.53(C) sizes a jumper between electrodes by 250.66 as well — so a jumper whose sole connection is to the rods gets the 6 AWG allowance the same as a home run would. 250.53(C) sends jumpers to 250.64(A), (B) and (E) but not to (C), so a jumper need not be continuous with the GEC.

The GEC itself is different. 250.64(F): "The grounding electrode conductor shall be sized for the largest grounding electrode conductor required among all the electrodes connected to it." That reaches through the jumpers — an electrode tied to the GEC by a bonding jumper is still connected to it — so the GEC is a whole-system maximum, not a per-leg figure. There is no required order or hierarchy; 250.64(F)(1) says any convenient electrode. But the NFPA commentary is explicit that order changes the sizing: run the GEC to the ground rod first and then on to the water pipe, and that rod run is sized as if it went to the water pipe. So the economics run one way — land the GEC on the electrode that demands the largest conductor, and jumper out to the cheaper ones.

The arrangement picker follows 250.64(F): (F)(1) one GEC to a convenient electrode with the rest jumpered off it (pick which one and the legs re-size); (F)(2) an individual GEC run to each electrode, where only that electrode is connected to that run so its cap applies on its own merits; (F)(3) GEC to a busbar (¼ in. × 2 in. minimum, accessible, listed connectors or exothermic) with a jumper from each electrode; or one unspliced conductor looped through the lot, where 250.64(C) forbids necking it down partway so every leg is the maximum. Watch 250.70 in all of them — one conductor per clamp unless the clamp is listed for more than one.

250.24(D)(1) is also enforced: the grounded (neutral) service conductor is never smaller than Table 250.102(C)(1) requires (the same sizes as Table 250.66 for any residential service). A neutral raised by that rule is marked with an asterisk.

Feeder sets reduce the per-conductor ampacity required — e.g., 2 sets of conductors each carry half the load.

Managed Loads & Load Management

Ticking Managed on any load removes it from the generator demand while leaving it on the panel. The basis is NEC 702.4(B)(2)(b): where automatic transfer equipment is used together with a load management system, the standby source needs capacity for the maximum load the management system will connect — not the entire connected load. (702.4(B)(2)(a) is the alternative: size for the full transferred load. 702.4(B)(1) covers manual transfer, where the user manages the load.)

Every managed load is listed by name and VA on the printed report under Managed Loads — Excluded per NEC 702.4(B)(2)(b), so the exclusions are documented rather than invisible. Managed loads are also left out of Min. Service / Gen Feeder, the feeder and the neutral, because those results size the generator feeder. The utility service still carries every load, managed or not, and must be sized for all of them. The pool sub-feeder is the exception: it is always sized for its full load, because it carries that load on utility power.

Load management only justifies the smaller generator if a management system is actually installed and configured to shed those loads. If nothing sheds them, they belong in the demand.

Generator

The minimum generator kW equals the service demand VA ÷ 1,000. LP output and NG output differ per model — select the correct fuel source on the Summary tab.

Neutral Demand

NEC 220.61(A) defines the neutral load as the maximum unbalance — the maximum net calculated load between the neutral and any one ungrounded conductor. A load wired between two ungrounded conductors never appears on the neutral, so 240V loads (HVAC, water heater, electric dryer, cooking, pool feeder) contribute nothing. Only 120V loads count: Step 1 lighting, small-appliance and laundry circuits, plus any Step 3 appliance set to 120V, and the 120V control load of a gas dryer.

Cooking and dryer loads reach the neutral through Table 220.55 and Table 220.54, then the 70% of 220.61(B)(1). That allowance is permitted "where the maximum unbalanced load has been determined in accordance with Table 220.55 for ranges and Table 220.54 for dryers" — so the tables are what qualify you for it. 220.82 hands the neutral to 220.61, and 220.61(A) measures "the load determined by this article" — Article 220, tables included.

This is for the neutral only. The calculated load on the ungrounded conductors is untouched: cooking and dryers stay at nameplate per 220.82(B)(3)b and c, and the Step 5 total on screen is the nameplate figure. Note that Table 220.54 carries its own 5,000 VA per-dryer floor — that floor belongs to the table and applies wherever the table does, which is the neutral and nowhere else.

Table 220.55 is implemented in full: Column C, Note 1 (5% per kW over 12 kW for ranges of equal rating), Note 2 (averaging for unequal ratings over 8¾ kW, counting 12 kW for anything under 12), and Note 3 (the Column A / Column B alternative for appliances over 1¾ through 8¾ kW, applied per column and added). Where Note 3 is available and gives a lower result than Column C, it is used — 220.55 permits it "in lieu of the method provided in Column C" — and the report prints which path was taken.

Note 6 combines a counter-mounted cooking unit with not more than two wall-mounted ovens into one equivalent range. The note requires them to be "supplied from a single branch circuit and located in the same room," so every cooktop and oven on Step 5 carries a Room selector. Appliances sharing a room number are grouped and Note 6 is applied within that room; a third wall oven in the same room, or a second cooktop, counts as its own appliance. A freestanding range never joins the combination — the note treats one range as already being one range — which is why Step 5 keeps Cooktops, Wall Ovens and Ranges as separate lists. Give a second kitchen, a lanai or outdoor kitchen, or a casita its own room number — combining cooking loads that are in different rooms understates the neutral. Room affects the neutral only; the Step 5 nameplate total never changes.

The reduction in 220.61(B)(2) is applied: first 200 A at 100%, the portion above 200 A at 70%. That one is conditioned on the system type — a 3-wire single-phase ac service qualifies — not on the Part III tables.

220.61(C)(1) prohibits any reduction where the circuit is 2 ungrounded conductors and the neutral of a 4-wire, 3-phase wye system. This calculator assumes a 120/240V single-phase service; a dwelling fed from two legs of a 208Y/120 wye is not yet supported.
